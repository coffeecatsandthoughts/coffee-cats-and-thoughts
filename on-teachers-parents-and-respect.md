<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A few people have been asking if I'm going to write a 3am thoughts post about <em>Teach You a Lesson</em>.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The short answer is yes.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The longer answer is that I'm still working my way through the series. As of this writing, I'm on Episode 8, and while I already have thoughts about several of the episodes, Episode 5 is the one that refuses to leave my head. If you know me personally, you'll probably understand why.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Of all the episodes I've seen so far, it's the one that speaks most directly to something I've been thinking about for years. Long before PTA meetings, school events, parent concerns, and everything else that comes with having children in school.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>At its core, Episode 5 isn't really about a particular teacher, parent, or student. </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>It's about respect. Or maybe more accurately, what happens when respect disappears.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The minister of education spent much of the episode defending teachers, but what struck me was that he wasn't arguing that teachers are perfect.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>He wasn't saying teachers never make mistakes. He wasn't saying schools should never be questioned. He wasn't even saying parents should blindly agree with everything educators do.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>What he seemed to be defending was something much simpler: the belief that teachers deserve to be treated as professionals. The belief that educators deserve the benefit of the doubt before being publicly condemned. The belief that the people who dedicate their lives to teaching children actually know a thing or two about education.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Maybe that resonated with me because I grew up surrounded by people in the academe.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>My mother was a college professor. My aunts and uncles were educators. Some of the people I respected most growing up spent their lives teaching. I saw the hours they put in, the sacrifices they made, and the emotional investment they poured into students who weren't even their own children.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Teaching was never just a job to them. It was a calling.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That's why it has been difficult for me to watch how the relationship between parents and schools seems to have changed over the years. Somewhere along the way, partnership started looking a lot like suspicion.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A teacher corrects a student and suddenly the teacher is the problem. A child struggles academically and suddenly the school is failing. A difficult conversation happens and people start demanding accountability before they've even tried understanding the context.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Of course, there are bad teachers, </p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>But there are also bad professionals in every field imaginable. We don't usually assume every doctor is incompetent because of one bad doctor, or every lawyer is unethical because of one bad lawyer. Yet when it comes to education, people seem remarkably willing to judge an entire profession by its worst examples.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>What I've noticed, also, is that many parents seem willing to extend grace to themselves that they rarely extend to educators.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That might seem like an issue between adults, but children are always watching and listening.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>They hear the conversations at home. They hear how teachers are spoken about after school. They hear the complaints, the sarcasm, the mockery, and the constant questioning of a teacher's intentions or competence.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Then we wonder why some students struggle to respect their teachers.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Respect is difficult to teach when disrespect is being modeled. If a child repeatedly hears that teachers are unreasonable, unfair, incompetent, or out to get them, it shouldn't surprise us when that child eventually stops seeing educators as people worthy of respect. Children learn far more from what we demonstrate than from what we say.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Which is why I've always found it unfair when every challenge a child faces is automatically placed at the feet of the school.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>A child spends hours glued to a screen at home, but the teacher is expected to fix the lack of focus. A child refuses accountability, but the teacher is expected to build discipline. A child struggles with respect, resilience, or responsibility, and somehow the school becomes solely responsible for producing outcomes that require reinforcement at home.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The truth is that raising children has never been a one-person job.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Parents cannot do it alone. Teachers cannot do it alone. Schools cannot do it alone. The best outcomes happen when everyone understands they are on the same team.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>And maybe that's the part that hit me hardest in Episode 5.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The minister wasn't asking people to stop questioning teachers. He was asking them to stop treating teachers as enemies. There is a difference.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Questions can come from a place of partnership. Criticism can come from a place of concern. Accountability can exist alongside respect.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>What doesn't help anyone is approaching every interaction as though the school is something to fight rather than something to work with.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Of course, not everyone will take the same lesson from the episode. Some people watch the show and reflect. Others immediately start thinking about who it reminds them of. The funny thing is that sometimes the character they're looking for is sitting on their own couch.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The irony is that <em>Teach You a Lesson</em> spends a lot of time showing us the consequences of closed minds, yet the people who need that lesson most are often convinced the story is about somebody else.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Over the past year, I've found myself thinking about this more than I ever expected to. I've met parents who genuinely want to collaborate, solve problems, and make things better for everyone involved. I've also encountered others who seem to approach every conversation already convinced that someone needs to be blamed.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>The difference between those two mindsets is enormous. One builds trust. The other builds walls.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>Beneath all the drama in Episode 5, what I heard was a reminder that education works best when parents and teachers see each other as partners, not opponents.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>At the end of the day, parents and teachers are trying to accomplish the same thing: helping children become the best versions of themselves.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:paragraph -->
<p>That goal becomes much easier to achieve when we stop assuming the worst of each other and start remembering that we're supposed to be on the same side.</p>
<!-- /wp:paragraph -->
